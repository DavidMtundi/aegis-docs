## Introduction

Not long ago, paying for something meant reaching for your wallet: pulling out a card, handing it over or swiping it yourself, waiting for the terminal, signing a receipt, entering a PIN, or sometimes showing an ID. The process worked, but it asked the user to stop, prove, and confirm.

Then the experience transformed.

- Cards no longer needed to be swiped; they could be tapped.
- Phones, watches, and wristbands turned into payment methods.
- Biometrics replaced physical signatures. A fingerprint, a face scan, or a quick tap became enough to authorize a transaction.

Behind the scenes, payments intelligence became more connected, devices could be identified, and verified credentials could be stored digitally and reused.

That's how trust systems evolve. As technology improves, the methods people rely on to prove and confirm move from visible and manual to embedded and adaptive.

Identity verification is now going through the same kind of transformation. For years, KYC checks have largely followed a manual model: upload a document, take a selfie, wait for approval. Like early card payments, KYC asked users to stop, prove, and confirm. But that model is starting to break down.

This guide explores the shifts reshaping identity verification and what they mean for the future of KYC.

## Chapter 1 — Why KYC is under pressure

KYC is under pressure because the conditions it was originally designed for no longer exist.

It came into a slower world where customers were onboarded in person, fraud was easier to spot, and losses didn't happen in minutes, so suspicious activity could be investigated manually. Then KYC slowly adapted to the digital age, where customer onboarding could be done remotely, anywhere in the world.

But the social and technological changes of the 2020s changed the KYC paradigm. Regulators began asking not just whether fraud was prevented, but how every decision was made. At the same time, people grew more aware of the value of their personal data, making trust and transparency critical to whether they chose to engage with a business at all. Meanwhile, fraudsters started using automation to test attacks at scale, probing for weak points and exploiting them before controls could react.

### The institutional perspective

**Regulatory expectations are becoming more nuanced.**
Supervisors are no longer focused only on whether fraud, money laundering (ML), and terrorist financing (TF) are prevented. They are asking how decisions are made, whether outcomes are consistent, and whether decisions can be explained and audited later. Requirements around digital identity, AML/CTF, and online safety push organizations to build systems that are not only effective, but auditable and defensible.

**Global operations expose fragmentation in KYC systems.**
A PwC survey shows that 85% of organizations feel compliance requirements have become more complex in the past three years, with half of them operating across multiple jurisdictions. As organizations scale across regions, they deal with different regulatory requirements, data sources, and risk profiles. Many rely on a mix of local vendors, custom rules, and manual processes, which creates gaps. The same user may be approved in one market and rejected in another. Decision logic becomes harder to trace, audit trails become fragmented, and operational costs rise as teams try to maintain multiple disconnected systems.

### The end user's perspective

**Many users associate KYC with friction.**
McKinsey & Company research indicates that willingness to open a new account digitally is around 74%, while only 38% of customers believe the digital route is secure. This points to a clear gap between willingness to use digital channels and reluctance driven by poor user experience. Identity verification sits at the start of that experience, shaping whether onboarding feels seamless and trustworthy or frustrating.

**Concerns around personal data are also increasing.**
A PwC survey shows that 83% of consumers care about how their data is used and protected. Increasing digital literacy, particularly among younger and digitally native users, drives greater scrutiny of how personal data is collected and handled. Users increasingly expect clear explanations of why their information is required, how long it is stored, and who it is shared with.

Lack of clarity affects behavior: according to Sumsub's 2026 consumer survey, 73% of users have previously declined to provide ID documents due to insufficient trust in the requesting service. Identity verification requires users to share some of their most sensitive information, making trust in data handling a critical factor in onboarding completion rates.

### The technological perspective

**Fraud is becoming more sophisticated.**
Sumsub's Identity Fraud Report 2025–2026 shows a 180% increase in sophisticated fraud, including advanced deception techniques, synthetic identities, layered social engineering, device or telemetry tampering, cross-channel manipulation, AI fraud agents, and AI-generated identities. These attacks are designed to pass traditional identity verification methods that rely only on document-based, static checks.

**Digital identity infrastructure is expanding.**
The European Commission's 2030 Digital Compass aims for 80% of EU citizens to use an eID solution by 2030. Similar initiatives are emerging globally, with governments and private-sector organizations introducing digital IDs, identity wallets, and interoperable credential systems. National digital identity programs in India, Singapore, and the United Arab Emirates show how verified credentials can be securely issued, stored, and reused across services.

## Chapter 2 — The five shifts reshaping identity verification and KYC

### Shift 1 — Fraud is becoming harder to detect

Fraud growth has not stopped, but the pattern is changing. The global identity fraud rate eased from 2.6% in 2024 to 2.2% in 2025. What's different is the composition of fraud: low-effort attempts declined, while more advanced methods took a larger share of the threat landscape.

#### Synthetic identities

A synthetic identity combines personally identifiable information (PII) with fictional data to fabricate a person or entity. That can include primary identity elements such as name, date of birth, and government identifiers, plus supplemental elements such as address, phone number, or email.

This type of fraud targets the sign-up step. At sign-up, synthetic profiles can appear legitimate on the surface, allowing them to pass early checks before more advanced controls are triggered. Increasingly, synthetic identities are "aged" over time — used for low-risk actions, building transaction history, and passing initial reviews — which makes them look more trustworthy in risk models and less likely to trigger scrutiny. By the time larger cash-outs or money laundering attempts occur, the identity has a seemingly legitimate history, increasing the chance it moves through controls without being flagged.

**How synthetic identities are built**

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Real data | Leaked or publicly available personal data, plus a real government identifier. The "credible" part that makes the identity look legitimate. |
| Fabricated data | Fake details created to complete the identity, such as name, date of birth, address, and email. |
| Supporting signals | Digital proof points that help the identity appear active and believable, such as phone number, device history, IP history, and digital footprint. |

When synthetic identities become harder to use, fraud operations often shift toward **money mules and drop accounts**. These involve recruiting real individuals or compromising legitimate accounts to move funds on behalf of criminal networks. Because the identity itself may be genuine, these schemes are harder to detect through onboarding checks alone and require visibility into behavior, linked accounts, and transaction patterns over time.

#### AI-generated and forged documents

Fake documents are still one of the main entry points into digital systems. In 2025, ID cards accounted for 72% of all fraudulent documents, and 2% of all fake documents detected were already AI-generated using freely available tools such as ChatGPT, Grok, and Gemini.

This attack affects the document upload stage. A fraudster can now generate or edit an ID or driver's license so that its visual structure looks plausible enough to pass template checks, and even some document-forensics checks. As a result, document verification needs to check document security features, cross-check data against external databases, and detect inconsistencies between the document, the user's biometric data, and their broader risk signals.

#### Deepfakes

Deepfakes are still a top-five first-party fraud type globally at 11%, and in some markets they are growing extremely quickly: 158% YoY in Singapore, 124% YoY in Canada, and 269% YoY in South Africa. This growth is driven in part by the democratization of deepfake tools, which almost anyone can now use at little to no cost, dramatically lowering the barrier to entry.

The important technical distinction is between **presentation attacks** and **injection attacks**, because they target different points in the verification flow:

- A **presentation attack** tries to fool the camera with something shown to it. A well-designed liveness check can still detect inconsistencies in movement, texture, or response to prompts.
- An **injection attack** interferes with the capture pipeline itself by inserting forged media directly into the session, bypassing the camera entirely. The person may appear to blink, move, or follow prompts, but the system is no longer evaluating a real capture. Liveness alone is not enough; additional signals like device integrity, session validation, and capture-source verification are needed to detect that the session has been tampered with.

> "Modern deepfakes can no longer be detected by the human eye alone. Fraud prevention now depends on real-time analysis across multiple signals, from document checks and device intelligence to fraud networks and deepfake detection."
> — **Nikita Marshalkin**, Head of Machine Learning, Sumsub

#### Agentic AI in fraud

Agentic AI refers to autonomous systems that can plan, execute, and adapt fraud attacks without constant human input. Instead of manually crafting a scam or submitting fake applications, a fraudster can deploy an AI agent to run the full attack loop, refining its approach in real time as it learns from each failed attempt.

Agentic AI introduces **feedback loops** into fraud automation. Each failed onboarding, rejected document, or flagged session becomes training data for the next attempt.

In practice, agentic AI fraud shows up in three key ways:

- **Social engineering becomes highly personalized**, with AI agents adjusting tone and responses to obtain the sensitive data that makes the attack possible.
- **Synthetic identity becomes more scalable**, with batches of applications generated and tested in seconds.
- **Once inside, AI agents behave like legitimate users**, moving through systems gradually to avoid detection and siphon sensitive data or money.

**Agentic AI fraud scheme**

1. The AI agent gathers data on previous breaches, public sources, and prior interactions with a system or platform.
2. It builds a target profile, including identity, behavior, and likely responses.
3. It executes the attack (for example an onboarding attempt or a phishing call).
4. It adapts the next attack based on the signals it received.
5. It iterates and scales the attack across thousands of parallel attempts.

#### Fraud networks

The most important context for all three advanced attack types is that they are increasingly deployed as part of fraud networks. Fraud networks reuse document templates, device environments, IP addresses, camera environments, similar backgrounds in selfies, mouse or keystroke signals, and behavioral patterns, often packaging them into fraud-as-a-service kits.

One application may go unnoticed. Ten applications from the same device, on the same network, with similar document structures and session behavior tell a different story.

KYC processes have traditionally relied on identity documents, proof of address, and liveness checks. These flows often assume that verifying the information a user provides at a single moment is sufficient to establish trust. Modern fraud networks are designed to exploit this. Multiple accounts may independently pass traditional verification yet remain linked through shared devices, behavioral patterns, or reused identity data. Without signals beyond user-provided inputs, these hidden connections remain difficult to detect, allowing coordinated fraud to operate undetected.

**Fraud networks leave hidden patterns.** In the report's illustration, four accounts have distinct names, emails, phone numbers, and appearances, but each shares two or more of the same underlying signals: device fingerprint, IP address, document template, a known selfie, and liveness background.

### Shift 2 — AI has moved into the core of verification

AI is changing identity verification from a sequence of isolated checks into a decision system that evaluates trust across multiple inputs, signals, and known risk patterns.

In traditional verification, each application is verified on its own merits. AI supports the shift by analyzing user-submitted data alongside internal and external signals, helping evaluate risk across multiple inputs and determine when additional verification may be required.

This matters because AI-enabled fraud now appears across documents, biometrics, and devices. Identity verification has to account for forged media and for presentation and injection attacks, and signals need to be evaluated continuously rather than treated as fixed.

> "The focus is shifting from 'point-of-capture' checks to continuous trust. Regulators now want to see how you manage risk across the entire customer lifecycle, not just at the front door."
> — **Arturs Linde**, Head of Fraud Prevention, Sumsub

#### Document analysis and forgery detection

AI in document verification is used to detect fraud that continuously evolves.

It still starts with structured information extraction: the system reads the document, captures fields, parses the Machine Readable Zone (MRZ), and maps data to the correct layout. On top of that, forensic analysis evaluates visual elements such as fonts, spacing, photo boundaries, and security features. Logical validation then checks whether all elements are internally consistent, including alignment between visible fields and encoded data.

What AI adds is resilience against evolving fraud patterns. New AI tools appear continuously, producing new and often better forgery variations. Detection models trained on historical data lose accuracy until they are updated, creating a lag between the emergence of a new forgery method and the moment it is reliably detected. Closing that gap depends on how quickly the system learns from new signals.

At Sumsub, document analysis is connected to other signals collected during the same session. Device intelligence, network patterns, and behavior signals provide additional context when assessing whether a document is legitimate. Even if a document passes visual and logical checks but is linked to other fraud patterns, the case is flagged and used immediately to refine detection. The model adjusts to new patterns as they emerge, reducing the window in which new types of document fraud go undetected.

#### Advanced biometric checks

AI is used in biometric checks to assess both the person in front of the camera and the integrity of the stream itself. High-quality deepfakes can replicate natural facial movements, lighting, and expressions, appearing indistinguishable from a real person to the human eye. Some are generated in real time, so the user appears to blink, turn, or follow prompts. That's why biometric checks also need to verify whether it's a genuine camera capture or injected or altered media.

- **Presentation attacks:** AI analyzes how the face behaves on camera — whether movements are natural, lighting stays consistent, skin texture looks real, and depth appears correct. It also checks whether the video comes from a live camera rather than a screen replay or pre-recorded clip.
- **Injection attacks:** the focus shifts to the session itself. AI looks for signs that the video feed has been tampered with, checking device, verification SDK, and feed integrity. It also looks at how the device behaves during the session, checking for emulation, remote control, or tooling used to manipulate inputs.

#### Dynamic risk scoring

Instead of focusing on user inputs alone, AI-based risk scoring evaluates the whole session. A user may present a valid document and pass a liveness check yet still look risky when device signals, IP reputation, typing cadence, or transaction intent are considered together. AI-based risk scoring runs in real time, combining device intelligence, behavioral signals, and historical risk indicators to continuously update the risk score and recommend actions such as **approve**, **step-up verification**, or **block**.

Dynamic risk scoring is one of the biggest operational changes in verification: the score is no longer a gate to onboarding, but a live, constantly changing trust signal that shapes the customer journey.

#### AI-assisted orchestration

AI is also changing how compliance, legal, and fraud prevention decisions are executed. Historically, risk teams moved from one dashboard to another: document validation in one tool, sanctions screening in another, and case notes in a separate system.

In unified or integrated technology stacks, AI acts as an orchestration layer. It gathers data from multiple sources, routes it to the right queue, and assists with next actions:

- A **low-risk** applicant may move straight through.
- A **medium-risk** applicant may be asked for a selfie verification or a second document.
- A **high-risk** applicant may be paused for analyst review with the relevant evidence already assembled.

AI also supports lifecycle orchestration after rules detect a change in risk or record status. For instance, when a document expires or risk escalates, automated workflows can trigger re-verification requests, route submissions, and update user status without manual intervention.

AI orchestration gives compliance, legal, and fraud teams a more consolidated basis for decisions. It also supports explainability and traceability through detailed logs, which is increasingly important as AI is used in higher-stakes environments.

### Shift 3 — Identity data is becoming reusable

A user who verified their identity last month to open a crypto account will likely be asked to upload the same passport, take another selfie, and wait for another approval when they sign up for a different platform next week. From a compliance perspective this makes sense: each organization has its own KYC obligations. From a user's perspective there's a gap: they can reuse basic information like name, address, and payment details across services, yet identity verification makes them repeat the most sensitive and time-consuming step each time.

That gap is closing. Verified identity data is increasingly structured so it can be reused, with appropriate controls, across platforms and institutions. Three models have emerged.

**1. Identity data reuse within a platform.**
An organization verifies a user once and reuses that record internally: when the same user applies for a second product, triggers a higher-risk transaction, or returns after a long absence. The user doesn't upload the same documents again; the organization re-assesses the existing record against the new requirement. This is the most widely adopted form and is standard practice in many regulated financial institutions.

**2. Ecosystem-based identity data reuse.**
A trusted intermediary holds the verified data and enables reuse across any platform within its network, with the user's consent. Reusable KYC is a practical example: instead of repeating the full verification flow, a user's previously verified data is retrieved and reused when they onboard with another service in the Reusable KYC network. The receiving organization does not rely on the decision of the providing organization; it applies its own rules and decides whether additional checks are needed based on its own risk thresholds. Through these networks, verification times can drop to around 10 seconds, and completion rates can increase by up to 30%.

**3. User-controlled identity data reuse through identity wallets.**
Verified credentials are issued by a trusted authority, such as a government or a bank, and stored in a user-controlled wallet. When verification is required, the user presents the credential without the recipient needing to contact the original issuer.

Sumsub ID is an example: users verified through any Sumsub-integrated platform can store their identity data in a Sumsub ID account and authorize reuse at any of the 4,000+ integrated platforms. With more than 10 million accounts holding stored identity data, completion rates increase by 30% and verification time is cut in half for users who reuse identity data.

This model is starting to take hold. Gartner® places it in early mainstream and recommends organizations prepare to support different digital and identity wallets for varied use cases: government-issued wallets for citizen identity, personal wallets holding banking, employment, and educational credentials, or wallets for digital assets and payments.

- **W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0** provides the technical foundation for interoperability.
- **NIST's latest digital identity guidance** includes a user-controlled wallet federation model.
- **eIDAS 2.0** requires all EU member states to issue EU Digital Identity Wallets meeting Level of Assurance High by the end of 2026. Seventeen large-scale pilots across Europe are already testing wallet-based verification in banking, healthcare, and public services.
- In **Singapore**, Singpass supports over 41 million transactions per month across 5 million users; government-issued digital identity is already the default for regulated onboarding.

For organizations operating across these three models, the practical question is not which model to believe in but which to support. Ecosystem-based reuse is available today and measurably improves conversion without sacrificing compliance. Wallet-based credentials remain nascent in AML-regulated KYC, with adoption progressing faster in government services, but integration timelines for EU-operating organizations are no longer hypothetical.

> **Innovation spotlight: Reusable KYC**
>
> Reusable KYC allows previously verified identity data to be securely reused across services and organizations, reducing the need for users to repeat full verification while still meeting each platform's compliance requirements. Through trusted Reusable KYC Networks, reuse can extend across broader ecosystems. Verification times can drop to 10 seconds or less, with up to a 30% increase in KYC completion rates.
>
> The receiving organization applies its own rules to assess applicant data, request additional checks, and validate data against its own risk thresholds. Reusable KYC turns identity verification into a shared, evolving dataset rather than a one-time event.

### Shift 4 — Verification is expanding beyond documents

Document verification still matters, but it is increasingly part of a broader verification model. The reason is practical: forged documents remain the most prevalent fraud type. This pushes organizations toward data-driven methods such as cross-checking against authoritative databases, verifying device-linked signals, and checking digital credentials directly.

#### Non-Doc Verification

Adoption was gradual at first, as trust in database-driven methods developed and coverage expanded. As official data sources became more reliable and widely available, Non-Doc methods moved from niche use cases into one of the fastest-growing verification methods. It confirms that an identity exists in the real world in three ways:

- **It improves data confidence** by checking user-submitted information against authoritative sources such as government databases, national digital identity systems, credit reference records, and bank account records. With access to over 3 billion verified profiles globally, it can validate identities across a wide range of markets.
- **It introduces adaptive verification paths based on coverage.** A customer in a mature data market may complete onboarding through database confirmation and liveness, enabling flows up to 6 times faster. A user from a country without a trusted source may be routed automatically to document-based or NFC verification.
- **It reduces friction** for users who can be verified through trusted sources by eliminating repeated document uploads. This recovers users who would otherwise drop off at the document step, increasing successful verifications by up to 35% while maintaining compliance.

#### NFC-enabled checks

NFC-based verification reads the chip embedded in ePassports, eID cards, and other electronic identity documents. It is commonly used when higher assurance is required, particularly in regulated or higher-risk scenarios. The user taps their device against the document; the system reads the chip data, verifies signatures, and compares the chip-held data to what the user submitted in the session. This gives a stronger signal on document authenticity and makes forgery significantly harder, as attackers would need to replicate both the document and the cryptographically signed chip data.

#### Digital identity credentials

Digital identity credentials issued by trusted authorities and presented through wallets or ecosystem exchanges further complement verification. The user can present a verified attribute such as name, date of birth, age, residency, or legal identity status. The platform doesn't receive the underlying verification data, only the fact that the attribute is verified, by checking issuer trust, credential validity, and the requested attribute. Benefits:

- **Privacy:** the user shares only what is needed.
- **Experience:** no re-uploading documents every time.
- **Assurance:** the verifier validates signed data from a trusted issuer.

> **Emerging trend: Age verification**
>
> Age verification is becoming a distinct requirement, driven by global regulation focused on protecting minors and vulnerable users online. Governments are introducing stricter rules around access to age-restricted content and services, particularly social media, adult content, and iGaming. Age checks are no longer a byproduct of full identity verification but a dedicated, standalone layer, depending on use case and risk. Platforms may use database checks, document verification, biometric age estimation, or reusable identity credentials.

#### Know Your Agent (KYA) verification

AI agents are becoming active participants in digital ecosystems. They can browse, submit forms, initiate transactions, and interact with APIs on behalf of humans. As agents move from assistive tools to autonomous actors, organizations need a clear way to understand who is acting and under whose authority.

Know Your Agent (KYA) extends identity verification principles into a context where actions are performed by software on behalf of users or organizations. It defines two connected layers of identity:

- **Machine identity:** the technical identity of the agent, including credentials, keys, and permissions used to authenticate and interact with systems.
- **Human identity:** the real person or organization that authorizes and is responsible for the agent's actions.

At Sumsub, this is addressed by binding AI agents to verified human identities. Agent activity is continuously evaluated through device intelligence, behavioral patterns, and session context. When risk increases, the system applies targeted verification, such as liveness checks, to confirm a real person is present and authorizing the action.

### Shift 5 — Regulation is accelerating the development of digital identity ecosystems

Global momentum is not limited to a single country or framework. Across regions, governments are introducing national identity systems, digital wallets, and regulatory standards that define how digital identity is issued, verified, and reused. Implementation varies, but the direction is consistent.

| Region | Scheme | Summary |
|---|---|---|

| North America | **Mobile Driver's Licenses (mDLs)** | State-issued digital IDs on mobile devices, accepted at TSA checkpoints and selected services. Stored in Apple, Google, Samsung, and proprietary wallets for consent-based sharing online and in person. |
| North America | **Google Wallet / Apple Wallet digital passports** | Wallet-held digital passport credentials. |
| Asia-Pacific | **Aadhaar** (India) | Biometric-based identification for over a billion people; authentication across banking, telecoms, and government services. |
| Asia-Pacific | **Singpass** (Singapore) | Digital identity for government and private services: login, verification, and consent-based data sharing. 41M+ transactions per month across 5M users. |
| Asia-Pacific | **VNeID** (Vietnam) | Government-backed digital ID app integrating identity, public services, and authentication of digital transactions. |
| Asia-Pacific | **ThaID** (Thailand) | Digital identity verification for public- and private-sector digital transactions. |
| Asia-Pacific | **ConnectID** (Australia) | Government-accredited identity exchange; a hub in Australia's national digital identity ecosystem. |
| Asia-Pacific | **myID** (Australia) | Government Digital ID app with roughly 14M users for accessing government online services. |

| Africa | **Fayda** (Ethiopia) | Foundational digital identity with biometric authentication and eKYC for banking, telecoms, and essential services. |

These initiatives are building toward a broader digital identity ecosystem that extends beyond individual services. As regulatory frameworks, wallet standards, and identity systems align, digital identity will increasingly be reusable across platforms, with credentials issued by one entity recognized and accepted by another. For users, that means fewer repeated verification steps and more control over how identity data is shared. For organizations, it provides stronger, issuer-backed identity signals and a clearer audit trail.

## Chapter 3 — How these shifts play out by industry

The five shifts do not affect every industry the same way. The underlying direction is shared, but the operational pressures differ by sector.

### Financial services

**Fraud risks**
- Synthetic identity fraud now makes up 21% of first-party fraud, often entering at onboarding as clean-looking profiles that pass initial checks and are later used for lending abuse, mule activity, or staged cash-outs.
- Account takeover accounts for 19% of third-party fraud, appearing after onboarding through social engineering, credential compromise, or session hijacking.

**Regulatory challenges**
- Regulators expect organizations to show how decisions are made and governed: risk scoring must be explainable, consistently applied, and auditable over time.
- Obligations extend beyond onboarding. Continuous AML monitoring is expected, with clear escalation logic when behavior changes.
- Verification workflows need to move from one-time checks to continuous, risk-based, auditable processes.

**KYC strategies that work**
- Establish a strong onboarding baseline combining document verification, liveness detection, and database validation.
- Layer in device, behavioral, and network signals to detect patterns that may not appear at sign-up.
- Use dynamic risk scoring instead of fixed approve/reject outcomes so trust can adjust over time.
- Trigger step-up verification (additional authentication or targeted review) when risk changes.
- Treat identity verification as an evolving workflow rather than a single isolated check.

### Crypto

**Fraud risks**
- Fraud often begins at onboarding with forged or AI-generated identity documents. ID cards account for 72% of all fraudulent documents.
- Synthetic identities or mule accounts are used to move funds, conduct coordinated withdrawals, or obscure ownership across wallets. Network-based fraud is common, with linked accounts sharing devices, IP addresses, or behavioral patterns.

**Regulatory challenges**
- Platforms must manage both onboarding fraud and downstream financial crime exposure, with visibility into wallet activity, transaction flows, and linked accounts.
- Expectations include Travel Rule requirements for cross-platform transfers and tax reporting such as TIN collection and validation under frameworks like CARF.
- Firms must maintain traceability of decisions: why transactions were approved, flagged, or blocked.

**KYC strategies that work**
- Reusable KYC matters because users move frequently between exchanges, fiat on-ramps, and wallet services within a single journey.
- Combine document verification, biometrics, deepfake detection, and database checks at onboarding.
- After onboarding, use continuous transaction monitoring and behavioral analysis. Add step-up verification at higher-risk moments such as withdrawals, wallet changes, or large transfers, with network-level detection for linked accounts and coordinated fraud.

> "As the cryptocurrency industry matures, KYC is moving from a straightforward compliance process to being continuous and intelligence-led — combining frictionless onboarding with robust, state-of-the-art KYC is driving innovation in our space."
> — **Ashna Vaghela**, Chief Customer Officer

### iGaming

**Fraud risks**
- Almost 50% of all fraud is linked to generative AI, with AI-generated faces and documents used to pass liveness and open accounts.
- Synthetic identities are growing rapidly (+329% YoY), often combined with template-based approaches that create many variations of the same user.
- Fraud shows up as fake and multiple accounts for bonus abuse, with repeat-actor patterns where one user re-enters with slightly modified identities.

**Regulatory challenges**
- Operators must balance strict eligibility controls with low-friction onboarding.
- Age and identity verification are mandatory; regulators also focus on protecting minors, vulnerable users, and responsible gaming.
- The dual challenge: prevent abuse and enforce compliance without friction that hurts conversion and retention.

**KYC strategies that work**
- Verification must prevent repeat abuse, not just validate identity.
- Start with ID, liveness, and device intelligence at onboarding.
- Enforce one-account-per-user using device, behavioral, and network signals to detect re-entries and duplicates.
- Continuously monitor for linked accounts, shared devices, and suspicious patterns; intervene when clusters form.
- For higher-risk users or regulated markets, add Proof of Address to confirm location and support jurisdiction-based controls.

### Mobility

**Fraud risks**
- Risk is tied to physical access. Attackers use fake or altered driving licences at onboarding, increasingly helped by free AI tools; 2% of fake documents are already AI-generated.
- After onboarding, risk shifts to account sharing or unauthorized access, where a verified account is reused by someone else.

**Regulatory challenges**
- Platforms must verify eligibility to operate an asset, not just identity: licence validity, age requirements, and jurisdiction rules.
- If the wrong user gains access, the platform carries financial loss and liability. Decisions must be legally defensible after the fact, showing why access was granted at a specific moment, not just at sign-up.

**KYC strategies that work**
- At sign-up, combine licence verification, liveness, age checks, and database validation.
- After onboarding, support real-time access decisions using device, location, and behavior signals.
- Trigger re-verification when risk changes: device switches, unusual usage, or signs of account sharing.

### Marketplaces

**Fraud risks**
- Buyers: chargeback abuse accounts for 16% of first-party fraud; bot-driven card testing is becoming more frequent and scalable.
- Sellers: fake merchants, listings, and payout fraud, with accounts set up to extract funds rather than run legitimate businesses.

**Regulatory challenges**
- Growing pressure around platform safety and user protection, including scam prevention and age-restricted transactions in certain categories.

**KYC strategies that work**
- Segment and adapt verification.
- Sellers: stronger onboarding with individual and business verification and database validation before enabling payouts.
- Buyers: lightweight onboarding, with step-up verification at high-risk actions such as large transactions, repeated disputes, or unusual behavior.
- Adjust trust continuously with dynamic risk scoring across identity, behavioral, device, and transaction signals.

### Trading

**Fraud risks**
- Account takeovers, identity misuse, and coordinated abuse of withdrawal flows.
- Forged documents or stolen identities used to open accounts, then exploit market access to move funds or manipulate withdrawals.
- Multi-account abuse, bonus misuse, and mule activity across accounts or platforms.

**Regulatory challenges**
- Strict AML and customer due diligence obligations, with ongoing monitoring of account behavior, transaction patterns, and withdrawal activity.
- Reporting requirements (such as MiFID II in Europe), clear audit trails, and decision traceability showing how customer risk was assessed and why actions were taken.
- Cross-border activity and high-value withdrawals increase scrutiny.

**KYC strategies that work**
- Knowledge questionnaires before full verification to assess trading experience, financial understanding, and risk tolerance (appropriateness and suitability).
- Onboarding that combines document verification, biometrics, and database validation.
- Ongoing monitoring of trading and withdrawal behavior, with step-up verification at withdrawals, profile changes, or suspicious trading patterns.

Across all sectors, the pattern is the same: identity verification is becoming more layered, continuous, and tied to real-world risk. What changes is how the pressure shows up. The common response is a shift away from static checks toward orchestrated verification built on multiple signals and triggered at different stages of the customer journey.

## Chapter 4 — How to assess and upgrade your KYC strategy

This checklist assesses KYC as it actually runs — not what tools you have or what the process is supposed to be, but what happens when a user moves from onboarding to real activity. For each area, score **Yes = 2**, **Partial = 1**, **No = 0**. The maximum score is 20.

### 1. Is your onboarding process consistent at detecting and stopping synthetic or AI-generated identities?

- **Why it matters:** synthetic and manipulated identities are designed to look legitimate. If they pass, fraud scales into bonus abuse, mule accounts, and large cash-outs.
- **How to check:** review approved users later flagged for fraud. Did they pass onboarding without getting flagged? Which checks were applied?
- **Yes (2):** layered checks consistently catch suspicious profiles early.
- **Partial (1):** some checks exist, but there are gaps in detection.
- **No (0):** detection relies mainly on basic document or visual checks.

### 2. Can you link fraud signals across accounts?

- **Why it matters:** fraud networks reuse devices, IPs, and patterns. Without passive signals, you only see one account at a time.
- **How to check:** can you identify the same device across multiple accounts, or emulator/VPN usage? Were the fraud cases from step 1 linked only after the fact?
- **Yes (2):** device, behavioral, and network signals link activity across accounts.
- **Partial (1):** some signals are used, but detection is limited (for example IP checks only).
- **No (0):** passive signals are not used to link accounts.

### 3. Do you use dynamic risk scoring instead of binary logic?

- **Why it matters:** binary decisions either increase fraud (too loose) or kill conversion (too strict).
- **How to check:** does every session/user get a risk score? Does it update during the session or lifecycle? Can you explain which factors change it and how they are recorded?
- **Yes (2):** scores are calculated and updated dynamically from multiple signals.
- **Partial (1):** scoring exists, but updates are limited or only at certain stages.
- **No (0):** decisions rely on fixed approve/reject rules.

### 4. Do you monitor user actions after onboarding?

- **Why it matters:** risk evolves; real users can become risky.
- **How to check:** do you track login patterns, behavior, and device changes? Can you detect sudden shifts?
- **Yes (2):** continuous monitoring with automated alerts or actions.
- **Partial (1):** monitoring exists, but alerts are delayed, manual, or limited.
- **No (0):** no monitoring after onboarding.

### 5. Do you offer multiple methods of verification?

- **Why it matters:** not all users can verify the same way. Multiple methods improve completion and support different risk levels and regional requirements.
- **How to check:** can users verify via more than one method, such as documents, non-document checks, or Reusable KYC?
- **Yes (2):** multiple methods available.
- **Partial (1):** more than one method, but limited to certain users or regions.
- **No (0):** a single method.

### 6. Do you re-verify users and keep records up to date after onboarding?

- **Why it matters:** users may become higher risk, documents expire, or individuals may appear on sanctions lists or watchlists. Without re-verification and ongoing screening, outdated identities stay trusted.
- **How to check:** are users re-verified when risk changes? Are records refreshed when documents expire? Is ongoing AML/watchlist screening performed?
- **Yes (2):** re-verification and ongoing screening trigger automatically on risk events or record changes.
- **Partial (1):** periodic or manually triggered.
- **No (0):** verified once, no re-verification or ongoing screening.

### 7. Do your checks work as one system?

- **Why it matters:** disconnected tools create inconsistent decisions and slow reviews.
- **How to check:** do signals feed a single decision engine, or are teams switching between tools?
- **Yes (2):** all KYC, AML, and fraud signals feed a unified workflow with integrated case management and automated reporting.
- **Partial (1):** some systems integrated, others fragmented.
- **No (0):** siloed tools and manual reporting.

### 8. Can you reduce friction for low-risk users and increase it for high-risk ones?

- **Why it matters:** over-verification kills onboarding conversion and revenue.
- **How to check:** does the verification flow or action routing vary by risk level?
- **Yes (2):** steps adjust dynamically based on user risk.
- **Partial (1):** risk-based steps exist but are applied manually or inconsistently.
- **No (0):** the same flow for everyone.

### 9. Can your system act on risk signals without delay or manual intervention?

- **Why it matters:** if action is delayed, fraud can still succeed. Manual review often detects risk but fails to stop it in time, or loses legitimate business through lag.
- **How to check:** when risk signals appear, is action automatic — blocking a transaction, triggering step-up verification, or limiting account activity?
- **Yes (2):** immediate, automated actions.
- **Partial (1):** some automation, many manual steps.
- **No (0):** everything waits for manual review.

### 10. Can you explain historical KYC decisions when required by regulation?

- **Why it matters:** regulators expect explainability, not just outcomes.
- **How to check:** pick five random recent cases. Could you explain why each user was approved or each transaction blocked? Are decisions logged and traceable?
- **Yes (2):** fully traceable, with clear audit logs and supporting records.
- **Partial (1):** some documentation, but incomplete or hard to retrieve.
- **No (0):** not consistently documented or explainable.

### What your score means

| Score | Level | Profile | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–7 | **High risk** — outdated KYC model | Onboarding-only checks; high exposure to post-onboarding fraud; poor visibility into evolving risk | Fraud losses keep growing; manual review grows with the business; high regulatory/legal risk |
| 8–14 | **Transitional** — partial modernization | Some layered checks and monitoring; gaps in orchestration, scoring, or lifecycle coverage | Fraud detected but often too late; inconsistent UX; hard to scale; potential regulatory scrutiny |
| 15–20 | **Advanced** — modern KYC model | Continuous risk-based verification across the lifecycle; balance between conversion and fraud prevention; explainable, defensible decisions | Faster onboarding for good users; lower fraud losses; strong regulatory position; market trust |

## Chapter 5 — Identity verification beyond 2026

Identity verification is not disappearing, but becoming less visible and manual, and more continuous, adaptive, and embedded into digital services.

A user downloads a financial app and taps "Create account." There's no form, no request to upload an ID or take a selfie. The app asks one thing: "Share your verified identity?" The user approves.

Instead of several manual steps, the user brings a verified identity with them. The app checks who issued it, whether it meets its requirements, and whether the current session looks consistent. It all happens in the background, in seconds.

Later, the same user logs in from a different device. The app notices the change but doesn't act yet; a new device on its own is not enough. A few minutes later, the user tries to move a large amount of money. Now the context is different enough. The app connects the earlier device change with the current action and asks for confirmation. The user performs a quick biometric check and the transaction goes through.

From the user's perspective, verification appeared only once, at the exact moment risk was high enough.

That's where all these shifts converge. Modern KYC doesn't sit at the start of the journey anymore. It runs alongside it, continuously checking whether everything still matches the original, verified identity.

> "AI reshapes both offense and defense. Attackers gain deepfakes, synthetic IDs, and autonomous fraud agents; defenders gain behavior modeling, millisecond anomaly detection, and self-learning systems."
> — **Mick Amelishko**, AI Advocate, Sumsub

This is not a model waiting to be invented. Most pieces of modern KYC already exist. Identity credentials are already issued by many governments and stored in digital wallets. AI models already analyze documents, biometrics, devices, and behavior. Risk scoring already happens in real time.

Organizations that rely on collecting documents and making one-time decisions will be seen as increasingly outdated by users and exploitable by fraudsters. Organizations that rely more on background data than on user input can reduce friction and improve risk control by not trying to solve everything upfront.

**Practical takeaways:** reduce dependence on onboarding, use the signals of each session, and react when behavior changes, not just when a user signs up.

### Gaps worth considering (post-MVP)

1. **Network / link analysis.** The report treats shared signals across accounts (device fingerprint, IP, document template, selfie background) as the core way to detect fraud rings and mule networks. Aegis has customer relationships (§12), but no device- or session-level entities. Candidate: a `DeviceSignal` / `SessionSignal` ingestion contract plus a "shared attribute across N customers" rule type.
2. **IDV result ingestion as a risk input.** Aegis should accept verification outcomes from external providers (method used: document, Non-Doc, NFC, reusable KYC, wallet credential; liveness result; injection-attack or deepfake flags) as `External risk signals` in §30 rather than building IDV itself. This keeps §31's "do not hardcode a single provider" principle.
3. **Risk-triggered re-verification.** The report's lifecycle orchestration (document expiry, risk escalation → re-verify) maps to a new alert/action outcome. Today Aegis outcomes end at alert → case. Candidate: a `RequestReverification` action emitted to the tenant via webhook (§53 Integrations).
4. **Step-up actions, not just alerts.** The approve / step-up / block outcomes suggest Aegis rule results could carry a recommended action alongside the alert, with real-time pipeline latency budgets (§43) set accordingly.
5. **Aged synthetic identities.** Synthetic profiles build clean history before cashing out. A candidate future scenario for §51: dormant-or-low-activity account followed by a sharp volume or velocity jump.
6. **Know Your Agent (KYA).** Transactions initiated by AI agents on behalf of customers may need an `initiator_type` (human / agent) and an authorizing-identity reference on the transaction model. Worth an open item in §67.
